How to Read Candlestick Charts

Candlesticks summarize trading prices over a defined interval. They are evidence about that interval, not predictions.

Educational overview from The Stock Market: From the Ground Up, published by Crown & Lantern Press. Illustrative prices and scenarios below are fictional, not historical market observations or recommendations.

By the end of this guide, you should be able to: explain the concept in your own words, interpret the fictional example, identify at least one limitation, and decide what to study next.

The four prices in every candle

Each candle represents an interval, such as one minute or five minutes, with an open (first price), high (highest price), low (lowest price), and close (last price). The body spans the open and close. A wick or shadow extends from the body toward the high or low when those prices lie outside the body.

Fictional five-minute example

Imagine a fictional five-minute candle with an open of $20.00, high of $21.00, low of $19.75, and close of $20.50. Its body runs from $20.00 to $20.50. The upper wick reaches $21.00, and the lower wick reaches $19.75. The price range is $1.25; the positive close relative to the open is $0.50. The candle says nothing about how many times price visited those values.

Candle color and what it cannot reveal

Many chart platforms color a candle green or teal when the close is above the open, and red when it is below. Colors can be customized, so always read the values. One OHLC candle cannot prove the chronological order of the high and low, which orders executed inside the interval, or whether a particular stop would have filled.

Read a sequence rather than an isolated shape

Compare several completed candles: Are successive swing highs rising, flattening or falling? Are lows being defended or broken? Does the candle overlap a prior range? A change of timeframe can alter the apparent structure. A five-minute candle can contain conflicting one-minute movement, and neither timeframe alone proves the cause.

Use volume as context

Volume reports the amount traded during the interval, subject to the platform and data source. Increased volume may accompany significant participation, but does not independently prove buying pressure, successful breakout, or future direction. Be explicit about whether the chart uses real historical data, a delayed feed or fictional training candles.

Check your understanding

What did the example actually establish? Which observation would change your interpretation? What is still unknown, and when is No Trade the more defensible answer? Write a short response before reviewing additional information.
