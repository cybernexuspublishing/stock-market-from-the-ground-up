Bid, Ask and Spread Explained

The bid and ask describe current quoted interest in buying and selling, not the outcome of your next order.

Educational overview from The Stock Market: From the Ground Up, published by Crown & Lantern Press. Illustrative prices and scenarios below are fictional, not historical market observations or recommendations.

By the end of this guide, you should be able to: explain the concept in your own words, interpret the fictional example, identify at least one limitation, and decide what to study next.

Understand the two sides of a quote

A displayed bid is a price at which a buyer is willing to buy a stated quantity. A displayed ask, or offer, is a price at which a seller is willing to sell a stated quantity. The quoted spread is ask minus bid. The National Best Bid and Offer (NBBO) summarizes protected best quotations under applicable U.S. market rules, but venue coverage, quote timing and available size matter.

Fictional spread example

Imagine a quote of $10.00 bid and $10.05 ask. The displayed spread is $0.05 a share. Buying 100 shares at the ask and immediately selling 100 at the unchanged bid would imply a $5 difference before fees, changes in available liquidity and other costs. That is an illustration of spread exposure, not a forecast of an attainable round trip.

How order type changes the trade-off

A market order seeks execution but does not guarantee a particular price, especially when liquidity changes. A limit order restricts your acceptable execution price but may not fill at all. Stop orders have their own triggering and execution rules, and a triggered stop-market order does not guarantee the stop price. Think of fill certainty and price control as different dimensions.

The displayed quote can change

Bid and ask values update as orders enter, execute, cancel or move. A narrow spread with only a small displayed size can still involve meaningful slippage for a larger order. Fast-moving prices, volatility, trading halts and thin liquidity can change the practical cost. Quote color and Level II displays should never substitute for confirmed execution data.

Beginner observation checklist

Before modeling a position, record the bid, ask, displayed size, intended order type, and assumption about slippage. Then write what would cause you to cancel the plan. If the spread is large relative to your planned stop distance, the risk model may be too optimistic.

Check your understanding

What did the example actually establish? Which observation would change your interpretation? What is still unknown, and when is No Trade the more defensible answer? Write a short response before reviewing additional information.
