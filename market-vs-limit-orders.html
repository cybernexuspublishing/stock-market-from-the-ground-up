Market Orders vs. Limit Orders: Beginner Guide

An order instructs a broker what you want to do. It does not guarantee that the market will give you the price shown on a chart.

By the end of this guide you should be able to explain the concept, state an important limitation and identify information still needed before any real-world order decision.

What does a market order do?

A market order requests execution at the best prices reasonably available under the broker’s applicable execution procedures. Its main advantage is that it generally prioritizes execution over your control of the exact price. The price shown before submission may change, and the available quantity at one quote may be insufficient. A market order is not a guarantee of a particular fill, and it may be restricted or handled differently during certain sessions.

What does a limit order do?

A buy limit order specifies the maximum price you are willing to pay. A sell limit order specifies the minimum price you are willing to receive. It may execute at your limit or at a more favorable price, subject to the actual market and order handling, but it might not execute at all. This is the essential trade-off: price constraint is not the same thing as fill certainty.

Fictional example: a small displayed ask

Imagine a fictional stock last traded at $20.00. The displayed bid is $19.95 for 100 shares and the ask is $20.05 for only 50 shares. You want 100 shares. Seeing the $20.00 last trade does not prove you can buy 100 shares there. A market buy could fill some shares at $20.05 and the rest at a higher available ask, or encounter a different quote before execution. A hypothetical buy limit at $20.05 will not pay more than the limit, but the unfilled portion can remain open or be canceled under the order's instructions.

Marketable limits still have risk

A buy limit set above the current best ask can be marketable and may fill promptly within its price cap. The displayed ask, available size, latency and exchange rules can still change. Limit orders do not remove every risk, including missed opportunities, partial fills, adverse selection and canceled or unexecuted orders.

Spreads and time-in-force matter

The quoted spread is ask minus bid. Time-in-force instructions specify how long an order may remain eligible, such as day-only or good-til-canceled where the broker supports it. Extended-hours conditions, halts, liquidity and the chosen broker's rules may change execution. Never infer exact execution from a single OHLC bar.

Check your understanding

If a last trade is $20.00 and the displayed ask is $20.05, can a market buy be guaranteed to fill at $20.00? Why could a buy limit at $20.00 remain unfilled? Explain which kind of uncertainty each order type addresses, and where NO TRADE may be the best response.
