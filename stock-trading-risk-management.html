Stock Trading Risk Management Basics

Risk planning starts with the amount you can afford to lose, not the size of a potential gain.

Educational overview from The Stock Market: From the Ground Up, published by Crown & Lantern Press. Illustrative prices and scenarios below are fictional, not historical market observations or recommendations.

By the end of this guide, you should be able to: explain the concept in your own words, interpret the fictional example, identify at least one limitation, and decide what to study next.

Define the risk budget before the entry

A trade plan should establish a maximum modeled loss, an invalidation level, position size, order mechanism and reasons to cancel. This is a planning exercise, not a guarantee. A trading loss can exceed a planned stop-based amount because of gaps, slippage, market conditions, execution failures or fees.

Fictional position-size calculation

Assume a fictional account value of $10,000 and a planning risk budget of 0.5% ($50) for one hypothetical trade. A proposed long entry is $20.00 and a stop reference is $19.75, making the modeled price difference $0.25 per share. Dividing $50 by $0.25 suggests a maximum of 200 whole shares before fees, slippage or other constraints. The proposed position would have a $4,000 notional value. Account buying power, allocation rules and real execution can require a smaller size or no trade.

Understand reward-to-risk without overconfidence

Suppose an illustrative target is $20.50. The planned target distance is $0.50 versus $0.25 of modeled stop distance, suggesting a 2-to-1 price-distance reward-to-risk ratio before costs. That arithmetic does not tell you the probability of reaching the target, the order in which stop or target might be touched, or the expected return.

Model spread, slippage and worst-case limitations

A last-traded price is not necessarily an executable price. Include a spread assumption and consider a range of adverse fills. A stop-limit order can remain unfilled; a stop-market order may execute well beyond the reference. Account for commissions and fees where applicable, and do not model historical outcome using candles that were not yet visible at the decision pause.

A valid plan can end in no trade

Write the catalyst and evidence, intended direction, higher-timeframe context, entry condition, invalidation level, stop mechanism, profit targets, cancellation conditions and alternative scenario. If information or liquidity is inadequate, choosing Watch, Reject or No Trade can be the disciplined outcome.

Check your understanding

What did the example actually establish? Which observation would change your interpretation? What is still unknown, and when is No Trade the more defensible answer? Write a short response before reviewing additional information.
