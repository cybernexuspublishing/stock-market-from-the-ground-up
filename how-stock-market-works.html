How the Stock Market Works

Before reading a chart, understand what a share is, why people buy it, and how an order reaches the market.

Educational overview from The Stock Market: From the Ground Up, published by Crown & Lantern Press. Illustrative prices and scenarios below are fictional, not historical market observations or recommendations.

By the end of this guide, you should be able to: explain the concept in your own words, interpret the fictional example, identify at least one limitation, and decide what to study next.

A share represents ownership

A stock is an ownership interest in a company. Owning a share does not guarantee a dividend, profit, voting rights of a particular kind, or a future price increase; those features depend on the security and its terms. People can buy shares for potential long-term appreciation or income, but their market value can decline substantially, including to zero.

Two different places where shares change hands

When a company issues new shares to raise capital, the transaction belongs to the primary market. When investors buy and sell existing shares, they usually participate in the secondary market. A stock exchange provides a venue for displaying and matching certain orders, but transactions can also be executed through other regulated market structures. Your brokerage receives your order and may route it for execution rather than sending it directly to a single exchange.

The market price is a transaction, not a promise

The last traded price is the price of a completed transaction. The bid is the highest displayed buying price at a particular moment, and the ask is the lowest displayed selling price. A quote is not a guarantee that a new order will execute at that price or quantity. Prices move as orders, available liquidity, information and expectations change.

Fictional illustration: two investors

Suppose a fictional company's share last traded at $20.00. A buyer submits a limit order to purchase 10 shares at no more than $19.95. A seller currently offers shares at $20.05. Those prices do not match, so the buyer's limit order might remain unfilled. The buyer could change the limit, a seller could lower the ask, or other orders could arrive. The last trade by itself does not tell you what will happen next.

What a beginner should learn first

Learn what a share represents, what a broker does, how market and limit orders differ, why bids and asks matter, and why liquidity, risk and time horizon change an order decision. Do not assume a trade is appropriate merely because a ticker is popular or moving quickly.

Check your understanding

What did the example actually establish? Which observation would change your interpretation? What is still unknown, and when is No Trade the more defensible answer? Write a short response before reviewing additional information.
